# Capacity model — staging (B1ms), and what it means for production

Companion to [load.md](load.md). The arithmetic is generated by
[`scripts/load/capacity.py`](../../scripts/load/capacity.py), so every table below can be
re-checked, not just believed:

```bash
python scripts/load/capacity.py
```

```bash
python scripts/load/capacity.py --sku B_Standard_B2s
```

```bash
python scripts/load/capacity.py --pool 4 --overflow 2 --platform-pool 1 --platform-overflow 1
```

## 1. The binding constraint is Postgres connections, and the budget is 35, not ~50

Azure Database for PostgreSQL Flexible Server `B_Standard_B1ms` allows **50 connections, of which
15 are reserved** for replication and monitoring. That leaves **35 user connections**, per
Microsoft's limits table (learn.microsoft.com/azure/postgresql/configure-maintain/concepts-limits,
2026-07). The built-in PgBouncer is **not available on Burstable** tiers.

The connection-budget comment on `pg_sku` in `deploy/cloud/azure/variables.tf` counts against
"~50". Against the 35 that can actually be used, the row it calls "OK, tight" is over the limit:

| terraform comment | its total | vs ~50 (comment) | vs 35 (real) |
|---|---|---|---|
| steady: 1 app + 1 worker | 30 | OK | OK |
| during deploy: 2 app (old+new) + 1 worker | 45 | "OK, tight" | **EXCEEDS by 10** |
| app_min = 2 | 75 | EXCEEDS | EXCEEDS |

Peak connections per process are what the pools are *allowed* to open:

```
per process = NEXUS_DB_POOL_SIZE + NEXUS_DB_MAX_OVERFLOW + platform pool + platform overflow
            = 5 + 5 + 2 + 3 = 15           (common_env; one uvicorn worker per replica)
app         = replicas x 1 process x 15    (x2 while a rollout runs old and new revisions)
worker      = 1 process x 15               (the worker gets the same common_env)
rollout     = +1 connection for `alembic upgrade head` on the new revision's boot
```

- **Ceiling** is what Postgres must be able to accept, because under load every pool fills.
- **Expected** is what the read path actually holds:
  - the tenant pools saturated;
  - the platform pool unopened (it is lazy, and only staff routes use it);
  - the worker at its pool.

### Staging (`app_max = 2`)

| scenario | app processes | ceiling | expected | user limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| steady, 1 replica | 1 | 30 (OK) | 20 (OK) | 35 |
| rollout at 1 replica (old+new) | 2 | 46 (**EXCEEDS**) | 31 (OK) | 35 |
| scaled out to app_max=2 | 2 | 45 (**EXCEEDS**) | 30 (OK) | 35 |
| rollout while at app_max=2 | 4 | 76 (**EXCEEDS**) | 51 (**EXCEEDS**) | 35 |

### Production (`app_max = 3`, same SKU today)

| scenario | app processes | ceiling | expected | user limit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| steady, 1 replica | 1 | 30 (OK) | 20 (OK) | 35 |
| rollout at 1 replica (old+new) | 2 | 46 (**EXCEEDS**) | 31 (OK) | 35 |
| scaled out to app_max=3 | 3 | 60 (**EXCEEDS**) | 40 (**EXCEEDS**) | 35 |
| rollout while at app_max=3 | 6 | 106 (**EXCEEDS**) | 71 (**EXCEEDS**) | 35 |

**Only one app replica's ceiling fits beside the worker**, on both environments.

## 2. How that plays out under load on staging

- **Scale-out arrives with the load.** The app's `http_scale_rule` adds a replica at **50
  concurrent requests** per replica. So the second replica — and its pool — appears exactly when
  load is highest.
  - Its ceiling (45) is over 35.
  - Its expected read-path use (30) still fits, with 5 connections spare for everything else:
    staff routes opening the platform pool, the worker's sweeps, a manual `psql`.
- **A deploy during load is the most likely first failure.** Container Apps runs the old and new
  revision side by side until the new one is ready.
  - While scaled out, expected use is 51 against 35.
  - Postgres then refuses connections (`TooManyConnectionsError`, "remaining connection slots are
    reserved"). That surfaces as HTTP 500s under exactly the load that caused it.
  - It matches the 2026-08-04 laptop run, where every error was that exception.
- **Before connections: one event loop on 0.5 vCPU.** Each replica is a single uvicorn process
  (0.5 vCPU, 1 GiB) with 10 tenant connections.
  - Requests wait for a pooled connection (pool timeout) or for the event loop long before
    Postgres refuses anything. Latency climbs first, errors come later.
  - The large reads are the likely CPU cost: `/api/accounts` and `/api/contacts` return up to 200
    rows, and `/api/billing/entitlements` resolves every module on every page load.
- **B1ms is burstable.** Postgres CPU runs on credits.
  - A short run can finish on banked credits that a soak would exhaust.
  - Watch Azure Monitor `cpu_credits_remaining` beside every staging run; a latency cliff with
    connections below 35 is the credit balance, not the app.
- **The worker is capped by the same pools.** `run_worker` derives its concurrency from the pool:
  `pool + overflow − 5 reserve`. That is **25** on the 10+20 defaults the heartbeat was
  benchmarked on, but **5** on staging and production (5+5).
  - The measured 1.59 accounts/s per worker at 25 concurrent crawls becomes ~0.32 accounts/s at 5.
    This is derived, not measured: the loop scaled linearly with concurrency in that benchmark.
  - Shrinking the shared pools to fit connections would shrink it further (4+2 → concurrency 1,
    the old serial loop).

### Expected knee on staging — hypothesis before measuring

- **Latency.** p95 crosses the 500 ms SLO on one replica well before connections run out. That
  should happen once sustained demand approaches what one 0.5-vCPU process serialises.
- **Errors.** They should stay near zero until the second replica overlaps a rollout, or until
  staff traffic opens the platform pool while scaled out.
- **The number.** The capped runs in §5 measure where the knee is. The laptop's 25–50 VUs is not
  a prediction: four processes on laptop cores, a 3-endpoint path, and 1 s think time.

## 3. What to change first

In order. The first two are configuration; neither touches application code.

1. **Correct the budget and stop overcommitting — in the right places.**
   - Fix the `pg_sku` comment to count against 35.
   - Give the platform engine `1 + 1` instead of `2 + 3`: it serves staff routes only.
   - Size the **app's** tenant pool to what one 0.5-vCPU process can use concurrently.
   - But `NEXUS_DB_POOL_SIZE` / `NEXUS_DB_MAX_OVERFLOW` sit in `common_env`, shared with the
     worker. Shrinking them there silently starves the heartbeat, as shown below. Split them per
     container first.

   | what-if on B1ms | per app process | staging, rollout at app_max=2 (expected) | production, scaled to 3 (ceiling) | worker concurrency |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | today 5+5, platform 2+3 | 15 | 51 EXCEEDS | 60 EXCEEDS | 5 |
   | shared 4+2, platform 1+1 | 8 | 31 OK | 32 OK | **1** |
   | shared 3+1, platform 1+0 | 5 | 21 OK | 20 OK | **1** |

   Pools alone can fit B1ms only by starving the worker, and still not for a production rollout
   at 3 replicas (57 ceiling).
2. **Move staging and production to `B_Standard_B2s`**: 429 max / **414 user** connections.
   - Every row above fits with today's pools, including a production rollout at app_max=3
     (106 ceiling).
   - It is the same Burstable tier, an in-place compute change plus a restart.
   - `max_connections` does not follow a SKU change automatically: set it for the new size (the
     limits page says so explicitly).
   - Price it before deciding; this doc does not quote Azure prices.
3. **A connection pooler (PgBouncer) only after that**, and preferably built in.
   - Burstable has no built-in PgBouncer.
   - Self-hosting one as a Container App is another stateful hop in the VNet.
   - It needs asyncpg's prepared-statement cache off (`statement_cache_size=0`) in transaction mode.
   - It earns its place on General Purpose (built in), or when replicas × pools outgrow even B2s.
4. **Then tune scale-out to the knee.** If §5 shows p95 degrading well below 50 concurrent requests
   per replica, lower `concurrent_requests` so replica 2 arrives before users feel it. That makes
   connections bind sooner, which is why 1–2 come first.

## 4. Extrapolating staging numbers to production

- **Per replica, staging transfers to production 1:1.** It is the same container shape (0.5 vCPU /
  1 GiB, one uvicorn process), the same region (East US 2, deliberately), the same SKU, the same
  pools. A request mix that meets p95 at N concurrent users on one staging replica meets it at N
  on one production replica.
- **Production's extra replica adds no capacity on B1ms today.** `app_max = 3` looks like 1.5×
  staging. Three replicas' ceiling (60) and expected use (40) are both over 35, so the third
  replica can only add connection errors under the load that summoned it.
- **Until the SKU or the pools change, production's usable ceiling is the same ~2 replicas as
  staging's.** Staging's knee is production's knee.
- **Data skew is the part that does not transfer.** Synthetic tenants have a documented, modest
  distribution. A large production tenant makes the 200-row lists and the per-account signal
  queries heavier. Re-validate a knee that matters with `--data anon` (the anonymised copy)
  before quoting it as a production number.
- **Deploys.** Production rollouts at 2+ replicas exceed the budget on B1ms by construction. Until
  2 or 3 above land, deploying during peak hours is the riskiest routine operation.
