What a credit costs, and what we sell it for

NEXUS GTM prices a credit at roughly sixteen times what it costs us to serve. This document shows the working: where the number comes from, why each tier sits where it does, how the money is collected, and which parts of it we cannot yet evidence.

§1

Two caveats, before the numbers

Scope of the margin figure

These are gross margins on cost of goods sold — the third-party API spend attributable to a unit of usage. They exclude infrastructure, salaries and support. They are the right number for “does the metering model work”. They are not company margin, and nothing here claims otherwise.

Size of the sample

The observed usage mix comes from 18 usage events across 4 active workspaces. That is enough to see the shape of demand and sanity-check a price. It is not enough to forecast. §10 sets out what would change our minds.

The margin floor is enforced in code rather than in policy. validate_rate() refuses to store any price below 50% gross margin unless finance records an audited exception, and it runs on the seed itself — so a bad price cannot reach the database, even by deploy.

§2

Why credits, and not seats

Our marginal cost is driven by what a user does, not by the fact of the user. A rep running 400 enrichments a month costs us roughly forty times a rep who reads the dashboard.

Under per-seat pricing those two pay the same: the light user subsidises the heavy one, and the heavy one is unprofitable at the margin. Worse, the customer’s rational move is to buy fewer seats and share logins — suppressing the very adoption metric we would be selling on. Flat rate has the same defect, unbounded: one bulk enrichment run can consume more COGS in a week than the contract is worth in a year.

Credits align price with cost because they are cost, marked up. Each capability carries a rate card and a cost rate; the ratio between them is that action’s margin. The customer sees one simple currency. We see a per-action P&L.

What we deliberately did not do

We do not price per API call. A credit attaches to a capability — “draft an email”, “verify an address” — not to an endpoint. Endpoints move when we refactor; capabilities do not. Pricing against endpoints would let a routing change silently reprice a customer.

§3

What customers actually spend on

Seventy billable capabilities are defined. All fifty-five priceable ones now carry a rate card and a cost rate. Demand is not evenly spread across them, and the skew is the most important empirical finding in this document.

A gap that had been hiding

Thirty-three capabilities had no rate card. A capability with no rate card is metered and then rated at nothing: usage accumulates, quotas count down, and no revenue line is ever produced. It looks handled. The largest was ai.scoring — 4,090 runs, 98% of all agent activity, metered correctly and free. ai.tokens was in the same state: the hook for token-metered billing existed and had never been priced. A test now refuses any capability that is neither priced nor named as exempt, so it cannot recur by omission.

Three-quarters of all credit consumption is enrichment and verification — not AI generation, which is what the product leads with in marketing. Two consequences follow.

  • Price against data cost, not token cost. LLM inference is cheap and falling; contact data is neither. A model built around AI cost would be built around 14% of the bill.
  • COGS reduction must target enrichment, and it does. Shared company and people stores mean forty workspaces tracking one company pay for a single crawl, and a recorded “not found” is never re-purchased. Both attack the 40% line directly.

Note that a cache hit is still metered. The customer received an answer and is billed for the answer; what the shared store improves is COGS, not price. Billing only on a miss would hand the saving to whichever customer happened to ask second, and make revenue depend on crawl order.

This skew is also why we price off the blended figure of $0.00188 rather than the catalog mean of $0.00161: real demand leans to the expensive end.

Per action, at a flat rate, with two token-metered escape hatches. The case for flat pricing is a measurement, taken across 4,174 real agent runs: token use spreads widely at the extremes but tightly around the middle.

Two capabilities exist for where that stops holding. ai.tokens (0.01 credits per 1,000) covers work a customer can make arbitrarily large — long-context analysis, document ingestion — because the flat-rate argument depends on a bounded distribution and those have none. ai.premium_model (4 credits) covers frontier models, which cost an order of magnitude more than the default: charging the same for both means the customers who ask for the better model are subsidised by the ones who do not.

Break-even burn is how many credits a customer must consume before the plan stops making money. Every tier carries an order of magnitude of headroom over its allowance. That is the answer to “what if someone abuses it”: at these prices they cannot, without consuming fifteen to twenty times their allowance — and the entitlement engine blocks or rates overage long before then.

The volume curve is deliberate

Credits per dollar rise with tier. A rising curve makes upgrading rational for the customer, keeps our margin roughly flat in absolute dollars per account while revenue grows, and puts the customers who cost us most on the tiers with the most headroom.

Starter used to break this curve and has been fixed. It shipped at 17.0 credits per dollar — below Core’s 21.1 — so a Core customer was better off buying overage than upgrading. Seats are a gauge, not a counter — resolved from live membership rather than summed from events, because a sum would only ever climb and a customer could never get back under a limit after removing someone.

§5

Pay as you go

Two entry points with no monthly commitment, for the two customers a ladder always misses: the one evaluating us who will not sign up to a subscription, and the one whose usage is lumpy enough that any allowance is either wasted or exceeded.

PlanPriceAllowanceBilling
Pay as you go$0/mononeEvery action metered, invoiced monthly in arrears
Credit Pack$999/yr25,000Prepaid, valid twelve months

Pay as you go is the true metered plan. No base fee, no allowance, fifty-five capabilities each rated individually onto a monthly invoice. It is the lowest-friction way to start and the natural landing place for a self-serve trial that ran out.

The Credit Pack is the commitment-free annual. 25,000 credits at $999 is 25.0 credits per dollar — deliberately between Growth (25.3) and the entry tiers rather than at the top. A customer who prepays but commits to no monthly floor gets a modest volume discount, not the best rate on the sheet. Scale Annual, which does commit, gets 40.3.

The detail that nearly broke it

Overage is charged only where a quota is set. A capability with no quota reads as unlimited and is skipped. So a pay-as-you-go plan built the obvious way — clone a plan, set the allowance to zero — inherits unlimited entitlements and bills nothing, while metering happily. It would look correct until the first invoice came out at zero.

Two further traps surfaced by running it. Cloning a base plan’s entitlements covered five capabilities out of fifty-five, because base plans carry few explicit rows and the rest fall back to catalog defaults — so the first build billed for five things and gave away sixty. And zeroing every quota zeroed the seat entitlement, which is billed as a seat price rather than in credits, meaning the plan allowed no members at all.

At $0.040–0.0475 per credit, pay-as-you-go is priced at roughly the entry rate — the worst on the sheet, which is correct. Committing to a plan should always beat not committing, or the ladder has no pull. The customer trades price for flexibility, knowingly.

§6

Why Scale is $149

This is where judgement enters, so it is the section to push on hardest.

The gap is empirical. Our busiest workspace burned 3,056 credits. Growth includes 2,000. That customer would exhaust their allowance and hit overage every month — the worst possible outcome, because it converts a growth signal into a billing complaint. The next tier up, Professional at 4,000, leaves only 31% headroom over observed peak demand, which is thin for a customer whose usage is climbing.

5,000 credits gives 64% headroom over measured peak demand. That is the sizing decision, and it comes from the data rather than from a round number.

$149 comes from the curve. Interpolating credits-per-dollar between Professional (31.0) and Business (40.2) for a 5,000-credit allowance lands at 33.6 — which is $149. At $139 the tier would sit at 36.0, undercutting Business on rate while offering less. At $159 it would sit at 31.4, barely improving on Professional. $149 is the price that keeps the ladder monotonic, and a monotonic ladder is what stops customers gaming the tiers.

The annual trade, stated plainly

Scale Annual is $1,490 — ten months for twelve. A 16.7% discount for a twelve-month commitment sits in the middle of the conventional 15–20% band.

We give up 16.7% of revenue per customer to convert monthly churn risk into a twelve-month commitment, and to pull twelve months of cash forward. At our stage the cash timing is worth more than the margin: annual prepay funds the enrichment spend the customer will incur over that year, so working capital moves in our favour rather than against it.

At 40.3 credits per dollar the annual plan sits deliberately level with Business rather than beyond it. Annual commitment is rewarded with a discount, not with a better unit rate that would cannibalise the top monthly tier.

§7

How the money is actually collected

Application code calls one metering function and never mentions a plan or a price. Plans, quotas and prices are rows, not branches — which is what makes everything below configuration rather than engineering.

  • Self-serve subscription. The customer picks a plan in-product, goes through hosted Stripe Checkout, and the subscription reaches us by webhook — never by our own write. Card data never touches our infrastructure, so PCI scope stays at the provider.
  • Usage overage. Past the included allowance, the engine blocks, throttles, or rates overage per plan. Credits are pre-paid, so rating deducts what the period’s burns already covered — otherwise the customer pays twice for one overage. Corrections are compensating negative rows, never deletes, so the ledger stays auditable.
  • Metered invoicing. Usage is rated by us and raised as a real invoice at Stripe, with our line items, a hosted payment page and a PDF. Our rating stays the source of truth; the provider prices nothing. Collection is keyed by invoice id, so a retry cannot double-charge.
  • Pay as you go. No subscription and no allowance: every action is rated onto a monthly invoice for exactly what was used. Mechanically it is the overage path with the quota set to zero, which is why it needed no new billing machinery — only a plan whose entitlements all start at nought. See §5.
  • Enterprise invoice. See §9.

Dunning retries on a configured schedule and escalates to past-due. It never silently voids a debt.

§8

Selling only part of the product

“What if a customer wants three features and not the rest?” is a configuration change, not a build. It is the question the entitlement model exists to answer.

Eleven module gates are independently sellable: agents, API, calling, discovery, integrations, lists, network, outreach, plays, relevance, signals. Switching one off does three things at once.

  1. The navigation item disappears — or becomes an upsell for admins who can actually change the plan.
  2. The route is guarded, so the page is not reachable by URL, bookmark, or a link from a colleague on a richer plan.
  3. The server refuses. The first two are presentation; this is the boundary.
The rule that makes a discount real

A module gate that only hides a menu item is a discount with no cost saving. So each gate carries its dependent spend: signals carries the signal scans and inbox tasks, agents carries orchestration runs and AI chat, plays carries automated play runs. Disabling a module genuinely stops the money going out — which is what makes a cheaper plan cheaper to serve, not just cheaper to buy.

Worked example — Core at $19

Eight modules off, leaving the ungateable floor plus Lists and Relevance. Two deliberate exceptions: relevance scoring is not tied to the relevance module, and account refresh is tied to nothing. Both appear on the Accounts page, which every plan includes, and cascading the gates would sell a page with its point removed. The general principle: gate the module, never the thing that makes the included pages worth opening.

API-only customers

API access is its own gate, so it can be sold, withheld or priced separately at any tier. For a customer who wants only programmatic access the packaging is: API on, UI modules off, allowance sized to expected call volume. That is a custom plan, assembled from checkboxes, with no code change.

Six surfaces can never be gated by any plan, and a test enforces it: Dashboard, Accounts, Contacts, Members, Settings and Billing. Billing is the load-bearing one — gating it would lock a customer out of the only page where they could change the plan that locked them out.

§9

Enterprise

Enterprise is a different mechanism, not a bigger number on the same form.

A negotiated deal becomes a per-tenant plan: modules selected, price and allowance set. Only changed rows are stored, so a module we add to the base plan next quarter still reaches that customer rather than freezing them in the past.

Custom and enterprise plans are refused by self-serve checkout. A tenant admin must not be able to re-buy their own negotiated contract at whatever a public price row happens to say. They are invoiced instead — our rating, raised at Stripe with a hosted payment page, or sent on terms.

Many enterprise subscriptions have no payment-provider object at all: a contract signed on paper and invoiced never passes through a subscription lifecycle. Reconciliation therefore skips subscriptions with no provider id, so real drift findings are not buried under deals that were never meant to be there. And reconciliation reports — it never repairs. Which side is right depends on what the customer agreed to, and an automated writer would resolve that wrongly and destroy the evidence.

Grandfathering

Subscriptions carry frozen terms. Editing a plan must never reprice an existing subscriber: a rise applies to new business and to renewals we choose to move, never retroactively to everyone on the plan.

§10

What we don’t know

An investor should discount the following.

No churn or conversion data

No customer has yet completed a full billing cycle on a paid self-serve plan. The 16.7% annual discount is priced off convention, not off observed churn. If churn is low we are over-discounting annual; if high, under-discounting it.

COGS is not fixed

LLM inference cost is falling fast. Contact-data cost is not, and it is 75% of our burn. A data provider raising prices compresses margin on exactly the line that matters most. The mitigations are built — shared stores, negative caching, source databases read ahead of paid providers — but they are mitigations, not immunity.

What would change the ladder: a segment consuming more than 20,000 credits a month (justifying a tier above Business); enrichment COGS moving more than 30% either way; or observed monthly churn landing materially outside 3–7%, which would reprice the annual discount.

§11

What stops a bad price

For a finance reader these are the controls, and they are code rather than process.

ControlWhat it prevents
50% margin floor on every rateA capability priced below cost. Runs on the seed, so a bad price cannot arrive by deploy
Audited margin exceptionDeliberate loss-leaders stay possible, but never silent
Plan-level margin warningFlags a thin plan without blocking it — acquisition tiers and free are legitimately unprofitable
Every mutation audited, before and afterPrice changes are attributable and reconstructable
Prices are rows, not codeA pricing error is minutes to fix, not a release cycle
New plans start as draftsA half-configured tier cannot become purchasable the moment it is saved
Plan class set by the service, never the requestNobody mints an unlimited or internal plan by typing a string

Enforcement has both a kill switch and a shadow mode that evaluates every decision and records it without blocking — which is how we measured “what happens if we turn this on?” without turning it on.

A deliberate commercial bias

The engine is biased toward allowing. Unknown capability: allow. No subscription: allow. Engine raises: allow. A billing bug must degrade to giving service away, never to blocking a paying customer. At our stage the cost of a wrongly-blocked customer exceeds the cost of a wrongly-served one by a wide margin.

Every figure marked measured is drawn from the production database and is reproducible: cost per credit is a capability’s unit cost divided by its credits per unit; the blended figure is the share-weighted average across observed usage; plan margin is price less allowance times blended cost, over price.
